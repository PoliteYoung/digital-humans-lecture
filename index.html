[3:00–7:00] This is how games like Wukong build a character. Four steps. Each step is a different job. Often a different person. (Point to Modeling.) First, modeling. An artist sculpts the 3D shape, in tools like ZBrush or 3ds Max. The result is a mesh. A list of points, called vertices, connected into small triangles. It's hollow. Like a paper model. (Point to Texturing.) Second, texturing. How do you paint a 3D surface? You cut it open and flatten it into 2D. Like peeling an orange. This flat layout is called a UV map. Artists paint color and material on this flat image. Then it wraps back onto the 3D surface. (Point to Rigging.) Third, rigging and skinning. Rigging means putting a skeleton inside. Bones in a tree. Shoulder, then elbow, then wrist. Skinning connects the surface to the bones. Look at this arm. Each dot is a vertex. Teal dots follow bone one. Orange dots follow bone two. In-between colors follow both. The new position is a weighted sum of what each bone wants. That's the formula. See the elbow get thin when it bends? That's a known weakness of this simple method. Artists spend a lot of time painting these weights by hand. In Unity, you've seen this as the Humanoid Avatar. (Point to Animation.) Fourth, animation. Here an actor wears an Xsens motion capture suit. Sensors on the body track every joint, and the avatar follows in real time. You'll see motion capture again later in this course. So every step needs skilled artists, and lots of time. A top character can take weeks, even months. Look at the map in the corner. Representation: mesh. Driving: skeleton. → But what if the actor and the character have different bodies?

One Motion, Many Bodies

  • Problem: different proportions & bones
    → foot sliding, penetration, missed targets
  • Engineering: Unity Humanoid mapping, Mixamo
  • Learned retargeting: any skeleton → shared "primal skeleton" → any skeleton
  • Learned rigging: any mesh → skeleton + skinning, automatically
  • Beyond animation: humanoid robots learn from human mocap
Skeleton-Aware Networks — retarget across skeletons
Neural Blend Shapes — Mixamo rig (left) vs. learned rig (right)
Aberman, Li, Lischinski, Sorkine-Hornung, Cohen-Or, Chen. Skeleton-Aware Networks for Deep Motion Retargeting. SIGGRAPH 2020. · Li, Aberman, Hanocka, Liu, Sorkine-Hornung, Chen. Learning Skeletal Articulations with Neural Blend Shapes. SIGGRAPH 2021.
[7:00–10:00] Here's a common problem. We record a tall actor. But our game character is short and round. Can we copy the motion directly? Not really. The bodies are different. Different height. Different arm length. Sometimes a different number of bones. So things break. The feet slide on the floor. The hands go through the body. A hand that should touch a cup misses it. Moving a motion from one body to another is called motion retargeting. In practice, engineers use tools. Unity's Humanoid system maps every skeleton to one standard skeleton. Mixamo does it online. Researchers asked: can we learn it instead? (Point to the top video.) This is Skeleton-Aware Networks, SIGGRAPH 2020, from Professor Baoquan Chen's group at Peking University, with Tel Aviv University. Different skeletons go into an encoder. They all land in one shared space, the "primal skeleton". A decoder turns it into any other skeleton. And no paired data is needed. The same group then learned the rigging itself. (Point to the bottom video.) This is Neural Blend Shapes, SIGGRAPH 2021. Give it any character mesh. It predicts the skeleton and the skinning weights automatically. Left is Mixamo's rig. Right is the learned one. Look at the shoulder and the elbow. Remember the thin elbow on our arm? The learned version bends much more naturally. And this is not only for games anymore. Humanoid robots learn from human motion capture. But a robot has different proportions, joint limits, and real physics. It can fall. So retargeting is now a key step in robot learning, too. → OK. SMPL: The Bridge

M(β, θ) → body mesh
  • β shape: tall, short, slim, heavy
  • θ pose: rotation of each joint
  • Still Linear Blend Skinning underneath
  • ML view: a low-dimensional shape space learned from thousands of body scans
  • One skeleton for all body shapes
Template → β gives the body shape → a sequence of θ drives the motion
Loper, Mahmood, Romero, Pons-Moll, Black. SMPL: A Skinned Multi-Person Linear Model. SIGGRAPH Asia 2015. · Video: official SMPL video, "Model Decomposition" (youtube.com/watch?v=kuBlUyHeV5U), dynamics column removed.
[12:00–14:30] SMPL is the bridge between hand-made 3D and learned 3D. It's a function. You give it two sets of numbers. It gives you a body mesh. Beta is shape. Tall or short. Slim or heavy. Usually just ten numbers. Theta is pose. The rotation of each joint. (The video plays on its own.) This is from the official SMPL video. On the left, the template body. In the middle, beta gives this person her shape. On the right, a sequence of thetas makes her move. So to drive a digital human with SMPL, you only need two things. One beta for the person, and one theta for every frame. (The original video also adds soft-tissue dynamics, the jiggle. We'll skip that today.) Underneath, it's the same skinning we saw on the arm. Nothing magic. The new part is where the shape comes from. Nobody sculpted these sliders by hand. They were learned from thousands of 3D body scans. So it's a low-dimensional shape space. You already know this idea. And remember retargeting? SMPL helps there, too. Every body shape shares one skeleton. So one motion works on all of them. SMPL is everywhere in research today. Pose estimation, motion generation, avatars. → But SMPL is a naked, smooth body. No hair. No clothes. No real face details. How do we get real-looking people?

[14:30–17:30] Now we capture real people. Both methods start the same way. (Point left.) These are photos of me, from our studio at DREAMS. Eighty cameras surround the person; thirty-two of them are color cameras. All cameras fire at the same moment, so we get one frozen pose from every angle. (Point to the top row.) The first idea: neural fields. NeRF, and later NeuS. We train a neural network to store the whole scene. You give it a point in 3D space. It tells you how far that point is from the surface. Negative inside, positive outside. That's an implicit representation. There's no mesh yet. Just a function. To use it in a game engine, we extract a mesh from it. Now we're back to triangles. (Point to the mesh.) This is the mesh we reconstructed from those photos. First the bare geometry, then the texture. The quality is good. But it's slow. Slow to train, and slow to render. (Click.) Then, in 2023, came 3D Gaussian Splatting. Same photos. Different representation. (Point to the bottom row.) I trained these Gaussians on the same studio photos: about ninety-five thousand blobs, half an hour on a laptop. Left: every blob drawn as a small ellipsoid. Right: the same blobs, splatted into an image. Forget the network. The scene is millions of small blobs. Each blob has a position, a size, a color, and a transparency. We render the blobs into an image, compare with the real photo, and use gradient descent. So it's like training a model. But the parameters are blobs. (Point to the last picture.) And this is the rendered result. In real time. That's why it took off so fast. Remember the title slide? Photo, Gaussians, rendered. This is how it works. Like a video. Video two was DEGAS. It's a 3D digital human. Think of it as a puppet. The Gaussians are attached to an SMPL body. So you can give it a new pose sequence, theta, and it follows. Look at the video again. The motion came from another person, a TED speaker. The man on the right never made those moves. So here's the test. Can it perform a motion this person never performed? DualGS: no. It can only replay what was captured. DEGAS: yes. And theta can come from anywhere: another person's motion capture, a motion library, or an AI model that creates new motion from text or speech. That's the power of a parametric body. Driving is separated from the data. To be honest: poses far from what was captured still look worse. Fingers, big bends, loose clothing. For an AI agent, we need the puppet. [21:30–24:30] Now let's put it all together. An agent that talks to you with a body. Five stages. The LLM writes a reply. Text-to-speech turns it into a voice. From the audio, we drive the lips and the face. Then we add gestures and body motion. Finally, we render the frame. Here's the catch. Every stage takes time. And the times add up. These numbers are only an example. But together, it's easily close to a second. In a conversation, one second of silence feels long. The common fix is streaming. Don't wait for the full answer. Start speaking the first sentence while the LLM is still writing. So which body should you use? It depends. Real-time dialogue, or VR: use a traditional mesh. It's the most mature. Unity handles it well. Offline content, like a marketing video: 2D generation is fine. A specific real person, high fidelity: reconstruction, like Gaussians. But driving it in real time is still an open problem. That's exactly what our next guest works on. → Before I hand over, one minute on responsibility.

[24:30–26:00] One minute on responsibility. This connects to the ethics part of your course. Likeness and consent. If you reconstruct a real person, whose face is it? Did they agree? Can they take it back? Deepfakes. The same tools that build a nice avatar can also fake a real person saying something they never said. Disclosure.
